Model comparison

The project deliberately used several models in distinct roles: one to plan, a different one to critique the plan, and two more to implement the same specification independently. This page reports what happened.

2. The critique round

The plan was handed to a second model with explicit instructions to attack it and to sort its findings into errors, omissions and preferences. It returned 14 findings. All 14 were accepted; two were implemented differently from the fix that was proposed.

The critic verified the physics derivations and all three worked examples by independent calculation, and they held — no equation changed as a result of the critique. What it did find was one error that would have actively misled an implementer, one that could have caused a wrong guard in the tool's headline interaction, and a systematic traceability failure between two documents.

The findings that mattered most

IDFindingOutcome
C-03 The plan said the worked example had "three complete risers, the third truncated" — which is self-contradictory. It has two complete risers plus a truncated third. Accepted. The serious part was downstream: an acceptance criterion demanded "3 complete risers", so a correct implementation would have failed the check and sent someone hunting a bug that did not exist.
C-04 The plan's wording implied that an absorption factor below 1 is infeasible. Accepted, and the risk was larger than reported. Checking all four presets showed A < 1 is reachable on every one. An implementer adding an A ≥ 1 guard would have broken the exact regime the tool exists to demonstrate.
C-01 Eight of ten cross-references between the plan and the decision log pointed at the wrong entry. Accepted. An artefact of writing the two documents in different orders and never reconciling them. Now verified programmatically.
C-05 The clamping functions returned inconsistent labels for which limit had been hit. Accepted, fixed differently. The proposed unification would have labelled a liquid-rate cap as a thermodynamic pinch. It is a user-interface limit, not physics, and telling a student otherwise teaches something false. The two concepts are now reported separately.
C-12 Add a demonstration preset just off A = 1, to exercise the numerical branch selection rather than only the special case. Accepted, value corrected. The suggested value sits exactly on the branch threshold and so takes the other code path — it would not have tested what it was meant to test.

Where the critic was wrong, and how that was caught

Two findings had correct conclusions supported by incorrect reasoning. One mislocated which operating limit produces a particular value; the other proposed a numerical value on the wrong side of a strict inequality. Both were found by recomputing the claim rather than reading it. The fixes were adopted and the reasoning corrected.

This is the most transferable lesson of the exercise: a critique is evidence, not a verdict. A second model is very good at finding things the author cannot see in their own work, and is also capable of being confidently wrong in the same breath. Checking it is part of the process, not a sign of distrust.

Your reading of the critique round. Was the critique worth the step? Did it find things you would have caught yourself? Did any finding change how you think about relying on a single model's output?
